Correct AI Usage

For me, using AI the right way means treating it like a study buddy, not an answer key. I still read the prompt, think about what I already know, and only then ask for help understanding the part I am stuck on. If it explains something clearly, I can retell it back in my own words before I move on, because that is the part that actually sticks. And I always keep a note of exactly what I pasted in and what came out, so if a professor asks, I can show the whole exchange instead of guessing what I did. 

You frame it well, but be careful with that "study buddy" idea - in a real grade, the
bar is whether you could explain the answer without the tool still open. Your retell-
back step is the strongest part you have and probably the one your professor would
actually want to see evidence of. The weak spot is that nothing you wrote keeps the
tool honest; a good rule would be to ask follow-up questions that force a different
answer and see if it is consistent the second time. And on the note-taking point: a lot
of students say they keep the exchange but then hand in a clean version, so keep the
messy transcript, not just a summary of it. Otherwise it reads like a solid everyday
ethic, not a test trick - it is really a habit, which is good, just do not let it
sound accidental.

Benefits of AI

  1. Speeds up repetitive work like summarizing notes or formatting data.
  2. Gives instant feedback on drafts, code, and study questions.

Who Benefits?

  1. Students who use it to check their understanding.
  2. Businesses that automate routine reports and customer questions.

How AI Tools Used

  • Brainstorming topics and outlining before writing.
  • Explaining error messages and concepts step by step.

Incorrect AI Usage

Submitting AI work as your own
Turning in an essay or code an AI wrote without citing it is plagiarism, even if you edited a few words.

Trusting AI without checking
AI tools can make up facts, sources, and statistics, so copying answers without verifying them spreads mistakes.

Sharing private data with AI tools
Pasting passwords, customer records, or other people's personal information into a chatbot can expose that data.

My honest take is that AI is one of those rare convenient tools that makes the middle of an assignment easier, and that is exactly the part where most cheating happens. The problem is not that students can now ask questions faster, it is that asking and answering have quietly collapsed into the same action. A tool that can do all of your thinking steps is not really a tool, it is more like a stand-in for the part of you that was supposed to do the work, and that is why I keep going back to the test from the AI critique above: could I still explain my answer later this month without the tool open? If not, then I was doing homework, not doing learning.
