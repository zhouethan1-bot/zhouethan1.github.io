What This Website Is About

To be honest, I did not really know what MIS stood for until this week, and once I looked it up it made a lot more sense than I expected. In my words, management information systems are the software and data a company depends on every single day to track customers, inventory, and money, and I found that explanation easier than most of the textbook descriptions we read. A retailer that runs promotions off of last month’s sales numbers is a real-life example of that, and Gartner keeps a running news page with articles about companies that do this well and the ones that get burned by it. MIT Technology Review has the kind of long-form pieces I keep sharing in our group chat, and their articles on where business AI is actually heading match what we are seeing in the slides. When I read through an MIT Sloan discussion about how data teams shape strategy, I realized how often the “right answer” is just a cleaner dataset. This site walks through that side of things and then shifts into AI, which for me lands closer to a daily habit than a one-off project, because I open a chatbot almost as often as I open a browser these days, and I think this class ends up mattering more than most of the intro courses I have had, mainly because it forces me to decide what I am actually allowed to do with these tools before a deadline forces that decision for me.

Management information systems (MIS) refer to the software, hardware, and data processes that businesses depend on to track operations, serve customers, and plan strategy. In simpler terms, they are the digital backbone that turns raw numbers into decisions, and retailers that use them well can spot a struggling product line weeks before it hurts the bottom line. Industry analysts at Gartner publish ongoing coverage of how companies succeed or stumble while adopting these systems, which is exactly why their research is worth following. MIT Technology Review approaches the same topic from the technology side, examining both the tools and the teams that build them. MIT Sloan adds the business perspective, looking at how data-driven thinking reshapes strategy instead of just reporting features. The point across all three sources, and the one this site is trying to land, is that systems do most of the heavy lifting, but human judgment is still what decides whether they help or hurt an organization. I want to leave this course able to explain that balance clearly, especially because the line between a tool and a crutch depends on how a person chooses to use it, not on the tool itself.
